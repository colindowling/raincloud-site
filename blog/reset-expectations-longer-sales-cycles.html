Your Sales Cycle Got Longer. Here's How to Reset Forecasts and Expectations

Colin Dowling November 2026 4 min read
Key takeaways
  1. A longer cycle is a planning fact, not a sales failure, once you can see why it's longer.
  2. Re-measure cycle length from your own closed deals, by deal type.
  3. Build pipeline further ahead and adjust coverage targets to match.
  4. Tell the board early, with the new math, instead of explaining misses later.

When sales cycles get longer, don't push harder on the same plan. Re-measure the cycle from your own closed deals, find what's causing the delay, rebuild your pipeline targets around the real timeline, and reset expectations with leadership early.

A leader I talked with recently, who sells large commercial projects, said the sales cycle in his space is long and he believes it's only getting longer. Some of his projects now run well over a year because of one outside approval step. Nothing his team does can make that step go faster.

That's an extreme case, but the pattern shows up in a lot of markets. More stakeholders. More scrutiny. More steps that sit outside the seller's control.

The tension: patience versus the board

On one hand, the board wants this quarter's number. On the other hand, the deals take as long as they take. It's been my experience that the more you can be patient with your pipeline, the better things go in the long term. But patience without a plan just looks like missing.

I land here: a longer cycle is fine if you can see it, explain it and plan for it. It's a problem when you discover it one missed quarter at a time.

Step one: measure the cycle you actually have

Most teams carry a cycle length in their heads that's a year or two old. Replace it with data.

  • Pull every deal closed in the last twelve months, won and lost.
  • Measure from first qualified meeting to signature, or to the loss.
  • Split by deal size, segment and product. Averages hide the story.
  • Use the median, not the mean. One monster deal can wreck the average.

Compare it with the year before. Now you know how much longer, and where.

Step two: find out why it's longer

A longer cycle has causes. Some you can fix, some you can't.

Things you can often influence

  • More people in the decision. Find them earlier and give each one what they need.
  • Slow internal approvals at the buyer. Ask about their process in the first or second meeting, not at the end.
  • Weak business cases. Deals stall in finance when the value isn't written down in the buyer's numbers.

Things you usually can't

  • Regulatory steps, permits or outside approvals.
  • Budget cycles that only open once a year.
  • Market conditions that make everyone cautious.

Don't spend energy pushing on the second list. Plan around it.

Many of these show up as deals that stall after the yes.

Step three: rebuild the pipeline math

A longer cycle changes when today's work turns into revenue. A deal created this month might close three quarters from now.

So work backward:

  1. What do you need to close, and when?
  2. What's your real win rate for each deal type?
  3. How long do those deals really take?

That tells you how much pipeline you need to create each month, and how far ahead. Usually the answer is more, earlier. That's a hard message, and it's better to hear it now.

Step four: reset expectations before the miss

Go to leadership or the board with the new picture before it shows up as a miss. Show the old cycle, the new cycle, the causes, and what changes:

  • When revenue is likely to land.
  • What pipeline creation needs to look like.
  • What you're doing about the causes you can influence.

A string of "it slipped" explanations erodes trust faster than one honest conversation.

Step five: change how reps run long deals

Long cycles need different habits. Make sure every deal has a next step on the calendar, not "I'll follow up." Track the last real contact, not just the stage. Agree on mutual milestones with the buyer, so both sides know what happens next and who owns it.

And don't let reps sandbag the forecast because they're afraid of long deals. Make the long deals visible and plan for them.

Signs your plan assumes an old cycle

  • Deals keep "slipping" one quarter, over and over.
  • Your pipeline coverage looks healthy, but this quarter's close rate doesn't.
  • Reps are surprised by approval steps late in the deal.

If that's you, the plan is wrong, not the reps.

Questions this post answers

Why are B2B sales cycles getting longer?

Common reasons include more stakeholders in each decision, tighter budgets, more scrutiny from finance, outside dependencies like approvals or permits, and buyers doing more research before they talk to sales. In some industries, regulatory or third-party steps add months. The fix starts with knowing which of these is driving your delay.

How do I calculate my real sales cycle length?

Take deals closed in the last twelve months and measure days from first qualified meeting to signed contract. Split them by deal size, segment and product, since averages hide big differences. Use the median rather than the mean, because a few very long deals can skew the number and mislead your forecast.

How much pipeline coverage do I need with a longer sales cycle?

Coverage depends on win rate and timing. When cycles lengthen, pipeline must be built earlier, because deals created this quarter may close two or three quarters out. Work backward from your target, your win rate and your real cycle length to find how much pipeline you need created each month.

How do I explain a longer sales cycle to my board?

Early and with data. Show the old and new cycle lengths, the causes, and what changes in your plan: timing of revenue, pipeline creation targets and any investment needed. Boards handle bad news better than surprises. A clear reset with a plan beats a string of quarterly misses explained after the fact.
