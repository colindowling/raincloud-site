<p>Here is a test you can run this afternoon. Open your CRM, pick the last ten deals your team sent a piece of your own content into, and see what they sent.</p>

      <p>In most companies the answer is nothing. The blog exists, the content calendar is full, somebody is measuring sessions, and not one asset has ever been attached to a live opportunity.</p>

      <p>That is not a distribution problem. It is a targeting problem, and it starts at the keyword.</p>

      <h2>Two audiences, and only one of them is buying</h2>

      <p>Most B2B content is written for the top of the funnel: broad, educational, aimed at people with a vague problem. "What is revenue operations." "Seven trends in enterprise sales."</p>

      <p>That content is written for someone who is not buying anything. It can still be worth producing, but it will not close a deal, and your reps know it, which is why they never send it.</p>

      <p>Bottom-of-funnel content is written for someone who has a budget, a shortlist, and an internal argument to win. They are not asking what the category is. They are asking whether they should pick you, what it costs, how long it takes, and what happens if it goes wrong.</p>

      <h2>The asset that works in both directions</h2>

      <p>The thing that makes bottom-of-funnel content unusually efficient is that the same asset does two jobs.</p>

      <p>A page that answers "how does implementation actually work" ranks for a low-volume, high-intent search &mdash; a handful of people a month, every one of them in a buying cycle. That is the inbound job, and it compounds quietly.</p>

      <p>The second job is the one people miss. That same page is what your rep sends on Thursday to the champion who has to defend the decision to a CFO on Friday. It moves a deal that already exists.</p>

      <blockquote>Top-of-funnel content is measured in traffic. Bottom-of-funnel content is measured in deals it unsticks. Only one of those shows up in a forecast.</blockquote>

      <p>This is why I treat content as one prong of a go-to-market motion rather than a marketing activity sitting off to the side. It makes outbound more credible, because a prospect who searches for you finds something substantive. And it gives sales something to send that is not a case study PDF from two years ago.</p>

      <h2>Where the topics actually come from</h2>

      <p>Not from a keyword tool. Keyword tools are good at telling you what is popular, which is precisely the wrong filter &mdash; popular means top of funnel, and top of funnel means people who are not buying.</p>

      <p>The topics come from your lost deals. Specifically:</p>

      <ul>
        <li><strong>Every objection that killed a deal in the last two quarters.</strong> If three prospects worried about migration risk, that is a page, and it is worth more than anything a volume report will suggest.</li>
        <li><strong>Every comparison your buyers actually ran.</strong> If they shortlisted you against two named competitors, those comparisons are being searched for. Someone is going to write them. It should be you.</li>
        <li><strong>Every question that appears in the third call.</strong> Pricing structure, implementation timeline, what the first ninety days look like, who owns it internally. These are unglamorous and they convert.</li>
        <li><strong>Every reason a deal stalled rather than closed.</strong> Stalls are usually an internal-consensus problem. Content that helps your champion sell inside their own company is the most under-produced asset class in B2B.</li>
      </ul>

      <p>Notice that all four sources are in your CRM and your call recordings already. The research is not the hard part. The discipline to write about narrow, boring, high-intent topics instead of chasing volume is the hard part.</p>

      <h2>What good looks like</h2>

      <p>A bottom-of-funnel page is closer to a sales document than a blog post. It is specific, it names things, and it does not hedge.</p>

      <p>It states pricing, or explains honestly why it cannot. It names the competitors and describes where they genuinely win, because a comparison page that concludes you are better at everything is read as marketing and discarded. It describes the failure modes of your own product. It gives real timelines.</p>

      <p>The instinct to soften all of this is strong, and it is exactly what makes most of these pages worthless. Your buyer already knows there are trade-offs. Pretending otherwise just tells them the page is not worth reading.</p>

      <h2>Why almost nobody does this</h2>

      <p>Three reasons, and they are all organisational rather than technical.</p>

      <p>It looks bad on a dashboard. A page that gets 40 visits a month and influences two deals will lose every reporting battle against a post that gets 4,000 visits and influences none.</p>

      <p>It requires sales and marketing to actually talk. The topics live in lost-deal notes and call recordings, which marketing usually cannot see and rarely asks for.</p>

      <p>And it requires saying specific things in public, which needs a decision-maker willing to sign off on naming a competitor or publishing a price. That is a founder-level call, not a content-calendar call.</p>

      <p>All three are why this remains available. The barrier is not difficulty. It is that the incentives inside most companies point the other way.</p>
