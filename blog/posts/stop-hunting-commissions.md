Reps who hunt the commission close less, because buyers can tell what they're optimizing for. The commission is the byproduct of doing the job well. The job is to educate, to help and to solve an actual problem for the client.

I became a much better salesperson when I decoupled how much money I made from the work I was doing. Commissions are a great scoreboard. But it took me time to learn that earning the commission isn't the job.

A sales leader I spoke with on the podcast recently said it better than I did: "Don't hunt the commissions, don't hunt the paychecks." Hunt what you need to do to provide a solution or service for your client. The commission happens naturally when you do that well.

## What commission-hunting looks like

It rarely looks like greed. It looks like normal pressure at the end of a quarter:

- Discovery gets shorter, because the rep already knows what they want to sell.
- Deals that don't fit get pushed anyway.
- The rep presents pricing before checking whether the deal can work.
- Hard truths about timelines and costs get saved for after the signature.

Every one of those buys a little speed now and costs trust later.

## Buyers have seen it before

The same leader works in a market where high commissions led to a lot of bad business on the consumer side. His answer on the commercial side: you have to be overly transparent.

Most B2B markets have some version of this. Your buyer has been burned by someone who sold them something that didn't deliver. They're watching to see whether you're the same.

## Check feasibility before you show the economics

One habit he described stands out. Before presenting the numbers, check whether the deal is even possible. In his words, you might present the economics, "but this might not even be financially feasible, let alone engineering-wise capable."

A rep chasing the commission shows the exciting numbers first. A rep solving the problem confirms it can be done, then shows the numbers. The second rep loses fewer deals late.

## What revenue leaders can do

- **Define the job.** Say it out loud: the job is the client's outcome. The number follows.
- **Review deals on fit.** In every deal review, ask what problem the client is solving and whether the deal delivers it.
- **Reward transparency.** Praise the rep who surfaced a risk early, even when it slowed the deal.
- **Make feasibility a stage.** Don't let pricing go out until the deal is confirmed possible.

## What to do this week

Pick your five biggest open deals. For each one, ask the rep two questions: what outcome is the client buying, and have we confirmed we can deliver it? If the answers are vague, those are your forecast risks.
