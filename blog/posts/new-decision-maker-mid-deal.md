When a new decision-maker joins mid-deal, treat it as a new opportunity. Meet them early, learn what they're trying to do and what they used before, and re-qualify the deal around their goals instead of defending what their predecessor agreed to.

A marketing leader I spoke with recently sells into a market where key buyer roles turn over often. New leaders come in used to certain old tools, or wanting to bring in the processes they ran at their last company. A deal that looked solid can change overnight.

Every seller has had this happen. The champion you spent months with leaves, or a new boss arrives above them, and suddenly the conversation is about a product you've never heard of.

## Why new leaders bring old tools

It's not stubbornness. A new leader has to show results quickly, and they're under pressure from the day they arrive. The fastest way to reduce risk is to use what they already know works.

Their old tools come with a track record they personally lived. Your product comes with a track record their predecessor believed in. That's not the same thing.

## The tension: momentum versus a reset

On one hand, you've done the work. There's a business case, a timeline, maybe a verbal yes. On the other hand, the person who has to own the result now didn't build any of that.

I land on accepting the reset. Pretending the deal is where it was just makes you look out of touch.

## What to do in the first two weeks

### Get introduced, don't cold-call

Ask your champion, if they're still there, to introduce you. If they've left, find someone else on the team who was involved. A warm intro beats an unsolicited email to a busy new leader.

### Offer help, not a pitch

New leaders are trying to get up to speed. Offer them something useful: a short summary of where the project stands, what was decided and why, and what's still open. That saves them time and makes you a resource instead of a vendor waiting for a signature.

### Ask what they want to accomplish

In the first meeting, listen more than you talk:

- What are you trying to get done in your first six months?
- What did you use before, and what did you like about it?
- What would you change about how things work here?
- What would make this project worth doing to you?

### Don't defend the old decision

Their predecessor's reasons may not be their reasons. If you argue for the old plan, you're arguing with their judgment. Instead, connect what your product does to the goals they just told you.

## Compete with their old tool fairly

If they bring up the product they used before, respect it. Ask what it did well. Then be specific about where you're different and where you're better for this company's situation, with proof from similar customers.

Sometimes their old tool really is a better fit. If so, say that. You'll be remembered as honest, and new leaders change jobs too.

## Build deals that survive turnover

You can't stop people from leaving. You can make deals less fragile:

- **Multi-thread.** Know several people, not one champion.
- **Map the committee.** [Give every stakeholder what they need](/blog/enable-every-buying-committee-stakeholder.html), so no single exit sinks the deal.
- **Write it down.** A business case in the buyer's numbers outlives the person who agreed to it.
- **Watch for signals.** Job postings, announcements and profile changes in key roles.
- **Ask early.** "How long has everyone on this project been in their role?" is a fair question.

In roles that turn over often, assume it will happen and plan for it.

## Signs a deal is about to reset

- Your champion stops replying, or starts copying someone new.
- A job posting appears for the role above your buyer.
- Meetings get pushed "until the new person is settled."
- Questions you already answered come back, worded differently.

## A short script for the first conversation

Keep it simple: "Congratulations on the new role. I've been working with your team on this project. I'd be glad to give you a five-minute summary of where it stands and what's been decided, so you can decide what you want to do with it." Then stop talking and listen. You're not asking them to keep the old plan. You're offering to save them time.
