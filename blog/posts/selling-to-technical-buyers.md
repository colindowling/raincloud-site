You sell to technical buyers by being curious instead of clever. Understand their problem better than anyone else in the deal, admit what you don't know, and bring in technical help with a clear brief. Faking expertise is the one move that reliably ends the conversation.

A go-to-market leader I spoke with recently, who's spent years selling to engineers, put it bluntly. Faking technical knowledge is absolutely a way to get shown the door, get laughed out of the room, and destroy your credibility.

I've sold to IT and engineering teams. It's been my experience that they're often just waiting to drag you into deep water and drown you. Not because they're mean. Because they've been burned by people who pretended.

## Why technical buyers test you

Engineers and technical leaders get pitched constantly. Most of those pitches are vague, overconfident or both. So they've learned to run a quick filter.

Somewhere in the first few minutes, they'll ask a question to see if you actually know what you're talking about. Sometimes it's a real question. Sometimes it's a trap.

If you answer with confidence and you're wrong, you're done. They won't say so. They'll just stop taking you seriously.

## You don't need to be the expert

Here's the tension. You want to sound credible. But you'll never out-engineer the engineer, and trying makes it worse.

I land here: your job isn't to know their stack better than they do. Your job is to understand the problem better than anyone else in the deal. What's broken, who feels it, what it costs, and what they've already tried.

That's something you can own. And it's what technical buyers rarely get from a seller.

## How to earn credibility instead

### Ask before you tell

Start with how things work today. Where does it break? What have you tried? What would good look like? Technical people usually like explaining their systems. Let them.

Anytime somebody shows up to show how smart they are instead of asking about the real pain, it doesn't work. That goes double with engineers.

### Use "I don't know" on purpose

When you get a question you can't answer, say so. Then say when you'll have the answer, and deliver it on time. Not knowing is fixable. Bluffing isn't.

Following up exactly when you said you would is often what earns the next meeting.

### Learn the vocabulary, not the deep end

Know your product's main concepts well enough to describe them accurately. Know the terms your buyers use. You don't need to explain the internals. You need to avoid getting the basics wrong.

### Bring technical help in at the right time

A sales engineer or product specialist is valuable, but timing matters:

- Too early, and discovery turns into a demo of things nobody asked about.
- Too late, and the buyer has already decided you're not serious.
- About right is after your first real discovery call, once you can brief them.

Give them a short brief: what the buyer is trying to fix, what they've tried, what success looks like, and what questions came up. Then let them go deep while you keep track of the business case.

In bigger deals, the engineer is one of several people to [enable across the buying committee](/blog/enable-every-buying-committee-stakeholder.html).

### Give them proof they can check

Technical buyers trust documentation, specific examples and things they can test themselves. Offer those instead of adjectives. A trial, a sandbox or a short technical doc does more than any slide.

## Make it a team habit

If one rep on your team bluffs, the whole brand pays for it with that buyer. So make it explicit. Tell your team it's fine, even good, to say "I'll find out." Review calls where technical questions came up and talk about how they were handled.

The reps who learn this tend to do better with technical buyers, because they become the one person in the process who isn't pretending.

## Signs your team is bluffing

- Technical stakeholders stop showing up after the first meeting.
- Follow-up questions go unanswered for days.
- Your sales engineer spends calls correcting what the rep said earlier.

Any one of those is worth a call review this week.

## Common mistakes

- **Answering a question you weren't asked.** Technical buyers notice when you dodge.
- **Bringing the sales engineer in cold.** Brief them first, every time.
