Why Deals Die After the Yes, and How to Surface Late Surprises Early

Colin Dowling November 2026 4 min read
Key takeaways
  1. A yes isn't a close. Most post-yes stalls trace back to something discovery missed.
  2. Map every party who can add cost or delay, including outsiders, early in the deal.
  3. Agree on a mutual plan with dates and owners after the verbal yes.
  4. Ask the 'what could kill this' question before the buyer has to.

Deals die after the yes because something nobody surfaced in discovery shows up late: an approval, a budget gap, or a third party who adds cost or delay. The fix is to map every party who can stop the deal early, then turn the verbal yes into a dated plan both sides own.

A leader I spoke with recently runs large commercial projects, and he described two ways deals die late. In one, the client says yes and then hits a bottleneck on their side that won't let the project move. In the other, an outside party shows up near the end and says, by the way, this will add a large cost nobody planned for. Projects die at that point.

His industry has unusual third parties. But every B2B seller has a version of this.

A yes is not a close

I'd argue the moment a buyer says yes is the most dangerous point in a deal. Everybody relaxes. The seller forecasts it. The buyer moves on to the next fire.

Then nothing happens for three weeks.

The buyer usually meant the yes. They just couldn't see every obstacle either. The question isn't whether surprises happen. It's whether you find them in discovery or at signature.

The two kinds of late surprise

Inside the buyer

  • An approval nobody mentioned, from finance, legal, security or a board.
  • A budget that was hoped for, not allocated.
  • A competing priority that just jumped the line.
  • A champion who left, or who had less pull than you thought.

Outside the buyer

  • A vendor, partner or contractor whose work the deal depends on.
  • A regulator, inspector or approval body.
  • An existing contract that has to end first.
  • A cost that appears only once someone outside looks closely.

Inside surprises slow deals. Outside surprises often kill them, because nobody in the room controls them.

Ask the uncomfortable questions early

Most of these risks are knowable. Buyers just don't volunteer them. So ask, in the first or second meeting:

  • Who else has to approve this, and how do they decide?
  • What has to be true outside your company for this to work?
  • What could add cost we haven't talked about?
  • What happened the last time you bought something like this?
  • What would make this not happen?

That last one feels risky. It isn't. The buyer already knows the answer. Asking it shows you're thinking about their success, not just your signature.

Turn the yes into a plan

When you get a verbal yes, don't celebrate yet. Build a short mutual plan with the buyer. One page is plenty:

  • Every step between now and go-live.
  • Who owns each step, on both sides.
  • A date for each.
  • Outside parties and when you'll hear from them.

Now a stall isn't a mystery. It's a date that slipped, and you know exactly whom to ask about it.

The same mapping is how you sell to a whole buying committee, and why longer cycles need a reset, not more pressure.

When it goes quiet anyway

Sometimes a deal still stalls. When it does:

  • Follow up with something specific. Reference the step in your plan, not "just checking in."
  • Offer help with the obstacle. A document for finance, a call with their legal team, an answer for the outside party.
  • Ask to meet the blocker. If your champion is stuck, the person causing it is the one you need.
  • Be honest about timing. If it's really paused, agree when to revisit, and take it out of this quarter's forecast.

Don't let a deal that's "not dead yet" sit in your forecast for months. That hurts everyone's planning, including yours.

Signs your deals are at risk after the yes

  • There's no date on the next step.
  • You haven't met anyone outside your champion's team.
  • Nobody has asked about outside parties, contracts or approvals.
  • "Legal is reviewing it" has been the status for three weeks.

What to do this week

Go through every deal with a verbal yes. For each one, write the next step, the owner and the date. If you can't, call the champion and build it together. Ask the "what could kill this" question on every call this week. You'll likely find at least one surprise early enough to deal with it.

Questions this post answers

Why do deals stall after the prospect says yes?

Common causes are internal approvals nobody mentioned, a budget that wasn't really allocated, legal or procurement steps, competing priorities and third parties whose sign-off or costs weren't known. The buyer meant the yes, but they couldn't see every obstacle either. Most of these can be uncovered earlier with direct questions about process and dependencies.

What is a mutual action plan?

A mutual action plan is a short shared document listing the steps both buyer and seller must complete to go live, with owners and dates. It covers approvals, legal review, security checks and outside dependencies. It turns a vague yes into a sequence of commitments and makes stalls visible as soon as a date slips.

How do you uncover hidden deal risks?

Ask directly and early: who else has to approve this, what could add cost, what happened the last time you bought something like this, and what would make this not happen. Include third parties such as vendors, regulators or partners. Buyers usually know the answers but won't volunteer them unless asked.

What should I do when a deal goes quiet after verbal agreement?

Reach out with something specific, not a check-in. Reference the next step in your plan and ask what's changed. Offer to help with the obstacle, such as a document for finance. If your champion is stuck, ask to meet the person who is blocking. If nothing moves, say so plainly and agree when to revisit.
