Stop Selling Features to Anyone Who'll Listen: Define an ICP You Can Actually Sell To

Colin Dowling November 2026 4 min read
Key takeaways
  1. Selling features to everyone means you're not really selling to anyone.
  2. An ICP is a short list of traits your best customers share, plus the situation they were in.
  3. Lead with the problem those customers had, not the feature list.
  4. A tight ICP makes targeting, messaging and hiring easier at the same time.

To define an ICP you can sell to, start with your best few customers, write down what they had in common before they bought, and then sell the problem those customers had instead of the feature list. If your pitch would work on almost anyone, it's not working on anyone in particular.

An operator I spoke with recently, someone who's been inside a lot of growing companies, said he can't tell you how many companies he walks into where they're selling a product by talking about features and attributes and throwing it at almost any customer.

Why "anyone could use this" is a trap

When a product can help a lot of people, it's tempting to sell it to all of them. It feels like a bigger market. It feels safer.

It isn't. A broad target means a generic message. A generic message means the buyer has to figure out on their own why your product matters to them. Most won't bother.

Here's the tension. Founders and sales leaders worry that narrowing the market will shrink the pipeline. In practice, a tight target usually grows it, because the people you reach actually recognize themselves in what you say.

What an ICP actually is

An ideal customer profile isn't a persona and it isn't a wish list. It's a short description of the companies most likely to buy, get value and stay. Keep it to things you can check:

  • Firmographics: industry, size, region, growth stage.
  • Environment: the systems they run, the team that would own your product.
  • Situation: what was happening when your best customers bought. A new leader, a failed project, a growth target, a compliance deadline.

That last one matters most. Two companies can look identical on paper, and only one of them has the problem right now.

How to build one in a week

Pick your best customers, not all of them

Pull the five to ten customers you'd clone if you could. Happy, profitable, easy to work with, got value fast. Ignore the rest for this exercise.

Find what they shared before they bought

What were they using before? Who brought you in? What triggered the search? How long did it take? Look for the pattern, not the exceptions.

If you can, call two or three of them and ask why they bought. Their words will be more useful than yours.

Write it in plain language

One paragraph. Something like: "Mid-market companies in this industry, with a small sales team on this CRM, who just lost a deal to the status quo and have a new revenue leader." If a rep can't read it and build a list from it, rewrite it.

Test it against your pipeline

Go through current deals. The ones that fit should be moving better than the ones that don't. If they aren't, adjust.

Then change the pitch

Once you know who you're selling to, stop leading with features. Lead with the problem that specific group has, in their language.

Instead of "our platform has automated workflows and real-time dashboards," try "most teams your size lose hours every week reconciling reports by hand." The feature is still there. It just shows up as the answer instead of the opening.

Anytime somebody shows up trying to show how smart their product is, instead of asking about the buyer's real pain, it doesn't work. Features prove you can solve the problem. They don't prove you understand it.

What a tight ICP fixes downstream

A clear ICP pays off everywhere:

  • Outbound lists get shorter, and replies tend to improve.
  • Messaging gets specific, so marketing and sales say the same thing.
  • Case studies get picked for the people who'll read them.
  • Hiring gets easier, because you know what kind of buyer reps will sell to.

It's one of those things that should be obvious, and the older I get, the more I learn things that should have been obvious years ago. Defining who you're for is one of them.

Signs your ICP is too loose

  • Reps build their own target lists, and no two lists look alike.
  • Your best and worst customers came in through the same pitch.
  • Win rates vary wildly by rep, and nobody can explain why.
  • Marketing and sales give different answers to "who's our customer?"

If two or more of those sound familiar, the ICP exists on a slide but not in the work.

Questions this post answers

What is an ideal customer profile in B2B sales?

An ideal customer profile, or ICP, describes the companies most likely to buy, succeed and stay. It covers firmographics like industry and size, the systems they use, and the situation that made them buy. It describes companies, not people. Buyer personas sit inside the ICP and describe the individuals involved.

How do I find my ICP if I have only a few customers?

Look at your best few customers, not all of them. Ask what they had in common before they bought: the trigger, the team size, the tool they replaced, how fast they decided. Interview two or three of them. Even five good customers usually reveal a pattern you can write down and test.

Why does selling features not work?

Buyers don't buy features. They buy a fix for a problem they already feel. A feature list asks the buyer to do the translation work, and most won't. Leading with the specific problem a defined group of customers has lets them recognize themselves quickly and decide whether the conversation is worth having.

How narrow should my ICP be?

Narrow enough that your message would sound wrong to someone outside it. If the same pitch could go to a hospital and a software startup, it's too broad. You can widen later. Starting narrow makes every part of sales sharper: the list, the message, the proof and the follow-up.
