How to run discovery with technical buyers

How to run discovery with technical buyers means letting them describe the constraint before you describe what you sell. That person might be an estimator on a job site or the engineer who will live with the software after the contract is signed. Start there. Your capabilities can wait.

Colin Dowling November 2026 6 min read
Key takeaways
  1. The technical buyer can throw the job out because the scope is wrong.
  2. Ask how the last number was built before you describe your work.
  3. Your seller owns the decision. Your specialist owns the constraint.
  4. The note after the meeting should name the constraint, not your menu.

How to run discovery with technical buyers is a different meeting from a tour of what you sell. The person across the table can throw the work out because the scope is wrong. Treat them as the person who has to build the job, install the system, or live with it after the signature.

Who is the technical buyer on the call?

The estimator at a general contractor who will not pass a number up until the takeoff matches the drawings. The facilities engineer at a food plant who knows which line can stop. The service manager at an IT firm who has to live with the choice after the owner signs. At a software company, the same seat is the engineer on the buyer's side who has to connect what you sell to the system they already run.

They are not an audience for a tour of your capabilities. They can make the job impossible.

What do you ask before you describe your work?

Use their words for the work.

How is this job scoped today, and who built the last number? Where was the last bid wrong, and which assumption moved the price? What has to be in writing before they will put their name on it? Who else can stop it, and what will that person ask?

Picture a commercial HVAC contractor sitting with a hospital's facilities engineer about a replacement. The meeting belongs to the shutdown window, the equipment already on the roof, and the season the plant cannot give up. Picture a software company sitting with the person who runs a logistics firm's warehouse system. The meeting belongs to what that system already does, what breaks when an order misses its window, and who supports the change after the contract is signed. In both rooms the feature list, or the service list, waits until those answers exist.

How should your seller and your specialist split the call?

Your seller owns why this job, why now, and who signs. Your estimator, applications engineer, or project manager owns the takeoff and the constraints. Send the specialist in alone and you get a quote with no agreement about the problem. Let the seller talk over the engineer and the buyer stops answering.

How to sell to technical buyers is the separate question of credibility when you do not know the answer. This page is the shape of the conversation. When several people can stop the job, map them the way you would any buying group: who signs, who lives with it, who can block it. The plant engineer is the blocker. If every one of these meetings is with a different kind of company, write an ICP you can sell to before you rewrite the questions.

What should you write down after the meeting?

One short note. The job. The constraint that changes the price or the date. The person who can say no. The question you still cannot answer.

If the note is a list of your services, the meeting was a presentation.

Questions this post answers

How do you run discovery with a technical buyer?

Let them describe the constraint, whether that is a bid, a line that cannot stop, or a system they already run. Tie that to price, schedule, and who else can say no. Your list of capabilities waits.

What should I ask an estimator, a plant engineer, or a software evaluator?

Ask how the current number or the current system was built, which assumption moves the price or the date, what must be in writing before they will put their name on it, and who besides them can stop the work. Use their words.

Who should speak on a technical sales call?

The seller owns why this job, why now, and who signs. Your estimator or applications engineer owns the takeoff and the constraints. One without the other is a quote with no agreement, or a conversation the buyer does not trust.

What should the note say after discovery?
